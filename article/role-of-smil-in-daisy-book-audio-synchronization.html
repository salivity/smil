Role of SMIL in DAISY Book Audio Synchronization

Synchronized Multimedia Integration Language (SMIL) serves as the navigational and temporal backbone of DAISY (Digital Accessible Information System) digital talking books. It bridges written content with audio by defining exact timing relationships between text elements and audio files—whether that audio consists of human-narrated recordings or text-to-speech (TTS) synthesized speech. Through SMIL, DAISY reading systems can simultaneously play audio and highlight the corresponding written text in real time, delivering a fully synchronized, multi-modal reading experience tailored for individuals with visual impairments, dyslexia, or other print disabilities.

What Is SMIL in DAISY?

SMIL is an XML-based W3C standard designed to coordinate independent multimedia streams into a unified presentation. Within the DAISY standard (such as DAISY 2.02 and DAISY 3), a book consists of distinct components: the structured text file (typically DTBook XML), the audio files, and a collection of SMIL files. The SMIL files do not contain the text or audio data themselves; instead, they function as an orchestrator, referencing text fragments and audio tracks to dictate when and how they play together.

How SMIL Synchronizes Media

At the center of SMIL synchronization is the <par> (parallel) element. The <par> tag instructs the DAISY player to present two or more media objects at the exact same moment. A typical entry binds a text segment to an audio segment:

  • Text Referencing: A <text> tag contains a URI pointing to a specific identifier (ID) within the textual XML document (e.g., a specific paragraph or sentence).
  • Audio Referencing: An <audio> tag points to the corresponding audio file, utilizing clipBegin and clipEnd attributes to define the precise start and end times of that segment down to milliseconds.

When a DAISY reading application reaches this <par> container, it displays the referenced text segment, visually highlights it, and plays the audio between the designated timestamps.

Recorded Audio vs. Synthesized Audio

SMIL handles both pre-recorded human speech and synthesized audio:

  • Human-Recorded Audio: Production tools capture narrator audio alongside a live text transcript. The software generates precise time-stamps for each sentence or heading. SMIL records these exact time boundaries, enabling seamless linear playback and sentence-level synchronization.
  • Synthesized Audio (TTS): When DAISY books rely on synthesized speech, audio may be pre-rendered into standard audio files (such as MP3) with SMIL timing generated automatically via text-to-speech alignment engines. Alternatively, in real-time TTS rendering, SMIL references structural elements to instruct the speech synthesizer on which text blocks to voice while managing highlighting and navigation states dynamically.

Enabling Granular Navigation

Beyond passive playback, SMIL is essential for user navigation. Because SMIL maps the text structure directly to audio timecodes, readers are not limited to fast-forwarding or rewinding. Users can skip forward or backward by sentence, paragraph, section, or chapter. When a user jumps to a specific heading in the table of contents, the reading system parses the SMIL file to find the matching entry point, immediately rendering the text on screen and queuing the audio at the correct millisecond.

Conclusion

SMIL acts as the connective tissue of the DAISY format. By maintaining a strict, standards-based timeline between written markup and spoken words, SMIL ensures that recorded and synthesized audio remain synchronized with visual text, establishing the accessible, flexible reading experience that defines digital talking books.
